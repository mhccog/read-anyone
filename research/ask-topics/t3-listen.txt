## t-listening | listening | How do I become a better listener?

n: vg-listening, vg-traps, vg-last-word, vg-reflection, ch-smalltalk, vve-triple-nod, vve-vocal-invitations
b: Good listening is mostly catching your own habits. Vinh lists five traps — judging, advising, one-upping, mind reading and daydreaming — and says spend a week just noticing which one is yours. To stop planning your reply while they talk, try his theatre game Last Word, Last Letter. Then show you're listening: Vanessa's slow nods and little "mm-hm… and then?" sounds invite more, and Chase says let your face react to the good bits. Finish with reflection: give back a key word they used, and let them go further.
say: "Hang on — 'stuck'? Tell me more about that."
tip: Repeat their word softly with a slight rise, then nod slowly and say nothing until they carry on.
short: Better listening means catching your trap, showing you're with them, and reflecting their words back.

## t-heard | listening | How do I make someone feel heard?
q: feel heard; make them feel heard; feel understood; validate; validation; empathy; empathise; show i understand; be supportive
n: ch-heard-seen, vg-reflection, vg-undercurrent, all-label-feeling, ch-verbal-mirror, ch-reaction
b: People feel heard when you show them what you heard, not when you say "I understand". Chase calls making people feel heard and seen the most important people skill there is. Vinh's method: notice a strong word, reflect it back, pause, then gently name the feeling as a question. Labelling a feeling, research suggests, can actually take the edge off it. Chase's verbal mirror — repeating their last few words — does the same thing. Hold off on advice; fixing too early tells them you've stopped listening.
say: "That sounds exhausting. Did it feel like nobody noticed?"
tip: Lower your volume, slow down, and let your face show a little of the feeling — then wait.
short: Make people feel heard by reflecting their words and gently naming the feeling, before any advice.

Vinh calls it radar: notice when their voice speeds up, slows down or goes cold, or their body changes, and ask about it gently. say: "You went a bit quiet there — is this an OK thing to talk about?"

## t-lying | leftout | How can I tell if someone is lying?

n: lo-lie-spotting, ch-baseline, vg-visual-radar

Chase and Vanessa both teach lie-spotting from the body, but Read Anyone leaves it out for that reason. What does work: notice a change from their normal, remember it usually means stress or discomfort, and ask an open, kind question. If trust really matters, look at facts and patterns over time, not a twitch or a glance.
say: "Something feels off to me — can we talk it through?"
tip: Keep your voice level and curious rather than accusing. Palms open, slower pace.
short: You can't reliably spot lies from body language, so notice changes and ask openly instead.

## t-angry | tough | How do I calm down someone who's angry?
q: angry; calm someone down; furious; shouting; yelling; heated; de escalate; deescalate; temper; mad at me; customer is angry
n: vve-calm, vg-calm-pace, vg-match-mirror-lead, ch-composure, ch-golden-bridge, all-label-feeling
b: Match first, then slow down — and never say "calm down". Vinh's technique: meet their pace and intensity for a moment so they feel met, then gradually lower your speed and volume; people tend to follow you down. Vanessa says name the feeling and invite more ("Can you tell me more about that?"), because suppressing anger tends to backfire. Chase adds composure: don't shrink and don't puff up — stay steady. When it's time to resolve things, give them a golden bridge, a way to step back without losing face.
say: "I can hear how frustrating this is. Tell me what happened."
tip: Start at their pace, then bring your voice down a notch with every sentence. Keep your hands open and still.
short: Calm anger by matching, then slowing down, naming the feeling and staying steady.

n: all-six-needs, vve-fears, vve-believe, vg-undercurrent, vg-traps
b: Acknowledge the weight before anything else. Chase's "hardship" need is a listening prompt here: people going through something often quietly wonder whether anyone sees how hard it's been. So say that you see it, then pause. Vanessa says make it safe to share fears without being judged, and later, if they're facing a scary step, four words help: "I believe in you." Vinh's listening traps matter most now — skip the advice, the comparisons and the silver linings unless they ask. Practical help, offered specifically, beats "let me know if you need anything".
say: "That's a lot to carry. I'm here — can I bring dinner round on Thursday?"
tip: Speak slowly and softly, keep the same warm tone through the hard part, and let silence be okay.
short: Support someone by acknowledging how hard it is, listening without fixing, and offering specific help.
