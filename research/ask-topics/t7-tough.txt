## t-disagree | tough | How do I disagree without a fight?
q: disagree; disagreement; argue; argument; conflict; push back; different opinion; debate; challenge someone; tell them they are wrong
n: all-disagree, ch-resonate, vg-situation-not-person, vg-yes-and, ch-golden-bridge, vve-high-stakes
b: Show you understand their view first, then add yours. Chase calls it getting into their river before you steer the boat: say back the best version of their point so they feel heard. Then use Vinh's "Yes, and…" instead of "yes, but…", and aim at the situation, not the person — "this plan", not "you". Vanessa's high-stakes approach is to name the goal you share, which turns it from a fight into a problem to solve together. Finish with Chase's golden bridge: give them a way to change their mind without losing face.
say: "I can see why you'd go that way — and I'm worried about one thing. We both want this to land, so can I share it?"
tip: Keep your voice low and steady, slow down on "and", and keep your hands open.
short: Disagree by restating their view fairly first, then adding yours with "and", aimed at the situation.

b: Apologise once, properly, for real mistakes — and drop the reflex "sorry" everywhere else. Vinh lists over-apologising as a low-status habit that makes people take you less seriously; his swap is to start with a pause, not an apology, or to say "thank you" instead ("Thanks for waiting" rather than "Sorry I'm late"). Chase's composure point fits: grovelling is collapse. When you do need to apologise, name exactly what you did, how it affected them, and what you'll do now — then stop. Vanessa notes that owning a small flaw makes you easier to warm to, not weaker.
say: "I got that wrong, and it cost you an evening. I've fixed it, and it won't happen again."
tip: Say it slowly and plainly, with eye contact. One clear apology lands; three nervous ones don't.
short: Apologise once, specifically, for real mistakes — and swap reflex sorries for a pause or a thank-you.
